\section{Bacterial Exotoxins}

Bacterial toxins are molecules produced by bacteria that can cause harm by interacting with host tissues or disrupting cellular processes.
Bacterial toxin forms are classified into bacterial endotoxins and exotoxins.
Endotoxins are known as lipopolysaccharides found in the outer membrane of Gram-negative bacteria and are released upon bacterial cell lysis.
Exotoxins, on the other hand, are proteins actively secreted into the extracellular environment by both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria \cite{sheehan2022bacterial}.
These secreted proteins have several modes of action that allow the bacteria to manipulate the host.
For example, some bacteria secrete extracellular enzymes that degrade host tissue or alter immune responses \cite{henkel_toxins_2009}.
Diphtheria toxin prevents protein synthesis.
Similarly, \textit{S. aureus} can overstimulate immune responses, while cholera toxin modifies second messenger pathways to aid bacterial survival and transmission without directly invading host tissues \cite{henkel_toxins_2009}.

Bacterial exotoxins are primarily classified as Type I, Type II, and Type III; although a fourth type has been proposed, it has not yet been fully recognized in the scientific literature \cite{Ghazaei2022}.
Type I exotoxins, mainly superantigens, do not enter host cells but instead overstimulate the immune system by triggering excessive cytokine release, leading to toxic shock syndrome.
Type II exotoxins, known as membrane-disrupting toxins, operate in two main ways. They either form pores in host cell membranes or they enzymatically degrade membrane phospholipids.
Type III toxins, commonly referred to as A-B toxins, are composed of an A subunit that disrupts intracellular processes and a B subunit that facilitates toxin entry by binding to specific host receptors \cite{Ghazaei2022}  \cite{Silbergleit2020}.
The proposed Type IV exotoxins operate by degrading the extracellular matrix. In general, exotoxins contribute significantly to bacterial virulence by impairing host immune responses, damaging tissues, and facilitating bacterial survival and proliferation \cite{kruger2024toxin}.

\section{Motivation}
The emergence of high-throughput technologies has improved bacterial toxin research by reducing the cost of sequencing bacterial genomes.
As bacterial genomes are being sequenced at an accelerating rate, the need for efficient annotation tools has emerged \cite{loman2012high} \cite{richardson2013automatic}.
Wet-lab annotation methods are inadequate for this volume of data due to their being time-consuming, costly, and error-prone.
Machine learning proposes a promising computational alternative to overcome these limitations.
Machine learning uses algorithms to get trained from data by recognizing distinct patterns in it \cite{batta2020machine}. Data can be either unlabeled, which is used for unsupervised learning, or labelled, which makes it convenient for supervised learning such as binary or multiclass classification \cite{sarker2021machine} \cite{alnuaimi2021machinelearning}.

The advancements in the classification of bacterial exotoxins had many scientific improvements in recent years, mainly with the applications of machine learning techniques.
Several studies have implemented machine learning for toxin classification, focusing on broad distinctions of bacterial exotoxins \cite{saha2007btxpred} \cite{beltran2021multitoxpred}.
However, most of them have focused on general bacterial classification.
They have also highlighted challenges for exotoxin classification, including small dataset sizes, issues with image quality, and the need for advanced feature extraction techniques \cite{automated2021bacterial}.


There still remains a significant gap in understanding if accurately distinguishing bacterial exotoxin among all types is possible.
Furthermore, the research community has not yet developed any predictor that distinguishes bacterial exotoxins based on the organism they target and invade.
Motivated by these gaps in the existing literature, this study aims to fill these gaps by building novel predictors: ExoType and ExoTarget.
ExoType aims to distinguish among five types of exotoxins based on two types of inputs: protein sequences and protein folds.
On the other hand, ExoTarget predictors are designed to predict the different targets of exotoxins based on two types of inputs: protein sequences and protein folds.

\section{Objectives}

This study has three main objectives, which primarily consist of the thesis's research question.
The first is to implement ExoType, a predictor for classifying bacterial exotoxin types based on their mode of action.
This includes five types of bacterial exotoxins: Type I (membrane-transducing), Type II (pore-forming), Type III (intracellular toxins), Type IV (ECM-degrading), and other types.
The second is to develop ExoTarget, a predictor for classifying bacterial exotoxin targets, differentiating between vertebrate and non-vertebrate targets.
The third objective is to evaluate and compare different machine learning models to identify the best-performing approach for the classification of bacterial toxins based on types and targets.
This objective investigates whether training models on bacterial exotoxin types and targets can achieve a competitive predictive performance with high \gls{MCC} scores.
