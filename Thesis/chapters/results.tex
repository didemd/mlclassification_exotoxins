
This section presents two predictors, ExoType and ExoTarget, which differentiate bacterial exotoxin types and targets based on different inputs, including protein sequences and protein folds. We first describe the data exploration and preprocessing steps, then outline the feature analysis, and finally discuss the model training and evaluation results.

\subsection{Data Exploration and Preprocessing}
In this section, we describe the data distribution among exotoxin types and targets, and explain the preprocessing steps—including redundancy reduction—that prepare the dataset for modeling.

In contrast, exotoxins categorized as “Unknown” comprise only 104 samples. Additionally, target classification shows that bacterial exotoxins targeting vertebrates (1,863 sequences) are three times more abundant than those targeting invertebrates (629 sequences). These observations highlight the need for mitigating data imbalance which will be observed in following model training step.

\subsection{Redundancy Reduction}

To ensure robustness and generalizability, redundancy reduction was applied to the exotoxin dataset. First, using MMseqs2 with a 100\% similarity threshold, duplicate sequences were removed, reducing the total count from 2,497 to 2,492. Next, applying a 30\% similarity threshold further reduced the count from 2,492 to 1,068. This reduction minimizes sequence similarity, allowing us to focus on the unique characteristics and functional diversity of exotoxins.

As shown in Table~\ref{tab:merged_dataset_distribution}, after eliminating redundant sequences, the over-representation of Type III exotoxins remains even more pronounced, which could increase prediction bias. Such imbalances highlight the need for techniques like oversampling or weighted loss functions during model training.

Therefore, generation of these non-redundant data allows further analysis of exotoxin properties such as sequence length and hydrophobicity.

\subsection{ExoType Predictor}

This section describes the development of the ExoType predictor, which classifies exotoxins into five categories: Type I, Type II, Type III, Type IV, and Unknown. The following subsections detail the training and evaluation of the model.

\subsection{Model Training}

To ensure robust evaluation and mitigate class imbalance, we employed 5-fold cross-validation with \texttt{class\_weight=balanced} and GridSearchCV for hyperparameter tuning. Initially, the preprocessed dataset was split into an 80\%-20\% training-test split to evaluate generalization on unique sequences. However, due to the imbalanced distribution of exotoxin classes, some models (e.g., KNN and Logistic Regression) exhibited overfitting, as indicated by a flat training curve in Figure~\ref{fig:split_test_learning_curves}.

In a second approach, the entire non-redundant dataset was used for training, and the previously eliminated redundant sequences were used as the test set. As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:import_test_learning_curves}, this approach improved the performance of KNN and Logistic Regression, while Random Forest and SVM maintained strong performance with smoother learning curves.

\subsubsection{Learning Curves for Protein Sequence-Based Embeddings}

While the first approach displays a more traditional generalization test, the second approach benefits from a richer training set at the cost of testing on potentially redundant data. Random Forest and SVM performed well in both cases, but the first approach led to greater variability in KNN and Logistic Regression, making them less reliable for handling unseen data.
